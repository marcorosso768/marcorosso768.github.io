research

My research develops new theoretical models of incentives, information frictions, and selection, and brings their implications to the data. Empirically, I build reproducible pipelines that combine administrative records, web scraping and text analysis, and geocoding to study political participation, crime, and labor markets.

The Tipping Point of Temptation: Prevention, Sanctions, and Selection into Public Service

SSRN | CRENoS Working Paper 26/15 · ISBN 978-88-6851-718-2
Corruption opportunities attract people who would exploit them and repel people who anticipate the cost of resisting them. I study both margins in a model of occupational choice with self-control costs. Three results stand out. First, when temptation is strong, small corruption rents can shrink the pool of entrants. Second, the public sector can lose integrity while it shrinks, so a stable or falling headcount can conceal a deteriorating workforce. Third, at equal net corrupt surplus, removing corruption opportunities yields weakly lower corruption than deferred sanctions, both at a given wage and with a fixed number of posts. When rents rise with the share of corrupt incumbents, the ranking carries over to steady states, and prevention can eliminate a high-corruption equilibrium that an equivalent sanction preserves.
occupational selection; self-control and temptation; corruption; deterrence; public-sector labor markets; multiple equilibria.
D73, J45, D91, H83.

Generative AI and Posted Labor Demand: Volume, Seniority and Timing

joint with Fabio Cerina and Simone Nobili
CRENoS Working Paper 26/13 · ISBN 978-88-6851-699-4
Featured in: Lightcast (October 2026)
We study how the release of ChatGPT affected posted U.S. labor demand, using 368 million Lightcast job postings (2016–2025) and a usage-anchored measure of LLM exposure. While we find a negative and causal effect on the volume of postings in AI-exposed occupations, we find no post-release effect on their seniority composition. After November 2022, posting volume in top-quartile-exposed occupations contracts by 9 log points (approximately 8.6 percent) relative to less-exposed occupations within the same metropolitan market and month. The estimate survives an extensive battery of robustness checks and is concentrated in measured LLM usage rather than AI capability. In exposed occupations, junior postings fall considerably more than senior postings after the release, but the divergence predates ChatGPT: it opens in 2021–22, during the COVID recovery, and shows no break at the release. The differential that a simple pre/post comparison would attribute to ChatGPT is absorbed by predetermined remote-work exposure. Our results caution against reading exposure-based entry-level declines as evidence of AI-driven seniority-biased technological change.
generative AI; ChatGPT; labor demand; seniority; job postings; difference-in-differences.

Who Misses the Election? Childbirth and Turnout within Couples

Request Draft
Childbirth is a temporary shock that hits two voters in one household. I ask how the resulting change in turnout is distributed within the couple. Using administrative turnout records for Bologna, where voting is in person on fixed days, linked to registers that connect both parents to the same birth, I compare couples whose child is born in the weeks before the polling days with couples whose child is born after. Couples whose child is born in the four weeks before the vote have 11.5 fewer recorded votes per 100; the probability that both parents are recorded as voting is nine points lower and father-only voting five points more likely, while mother-only voting differs little. Excluding the births most exposed to possible misclassification of hospital votes reduces these differences, and in the most restrictive sample only the father-only difference survives correction for multiple testing. Relative to the couple’s own pre-birth turnout, recorded participation is also lower in the month of birth, in a descriptive comparison that is sensitive to the same exclusions. From three months onward, the estimated mother–father gap is close to its pre-birth level.
voter turnout; voting costs; childbirth; couples; household; gender; political participation.
D72, J12, J13, J16.

Crime Perception and Voting Behavior: Evidence from Individual Data

joint with Giovanni Prarolo
This paper studies whether exposure to geolocated crime news shifts individual voting behavior in Italian elections. Using a panel of non-relocating Bologna voters observed across multiple election rounds, we exploit within-individual variation in exposure to nearby crime news during the pre-election month, holding constant individual and election fixed effects. Aggregate crime-news exposure yields weak and imprecise associations. Disaggregating by the offender’s perceived nationality reveals a sharp asymmetry. At national elections, exposure to immigrant-attributed crime news near a voter’s residence is associated with a within-individual decline of roughly 2 percentage points in support for the Five Star Movement—the largest party without a clear immigration stance during the sample period—and with a comparable rise in abstention; crime news attributed to Italian citizens carries no comparable association. The response is specific to crime content: comparable local non-crime news produces no analogous shift. These asymmetric responses, absent in aggregate measures, are more consistent with a nationality cue in crime reporting that activates an immigration-and-security frame than with a uniform reaction to crime salience. The evidence points to the attribution of crime in local news, rather than its mere occurrence, as the margin that matters for electoral behavior. A timing falsification supports the campaign-window reading but is sensitive to the offender-nationality classifier; we accordingly read the estimates as associations, with a cross-source replication under way to settle the causal interpretation.
crime; immigration; elections; news media; individual voting behavior.
D72, D83, K42, L82.

Proximity to Public Childcare and Voter Turnout: Evidence from Bologna

joint with Giorgio Bellettini, Carlotta Berti Ceroni, Martín Gonzalez-Eiras, and Giovanni Prarolo
Request Draft
Does proximity to locally provided public goods raise the political participation of the households they are meant to serve? We study Bologna, Italy, with administrative turnout records for all registered voters at four elections (2004–2013), linked to annual population registers, income, exact dates of birth, and a purpose-built geocoded census of childcare facilities. Our baseline follows parents with one co-resident child in the annual register as the child ages across service regimes, so that the relevant distance switches within person, and estimates separate distance slopes for local and national elections. The municipal-facility distance slope in local elections is statistically indistinguishable from zero, with a symmetric equivalence bound of 0.47 percentage points per 100 meters, or 1.25 points across the interquartile range of distance. The local–national contrast that the accountability hypothesis restricts is estimated less precisely: its bound is 0.79 points per 100 meters, and moderate effects on it remain compatible with the evidence. Proximity does not measurably moderate the parenthood penalty, no gender interaction survives multiplicity adjustment, and a switching cohort and six additional local amenities and services yield the same result. An earlier marginal contrast among parents with verified address continuity is not robust to specification and is reported as exploratory. The estimand is proximity: the estimates bound the mobilizing effect of living near a facility and leave the effect of obtaining a place in it unmeasured.
voter turnout; public goods; childcare; gender; local elections.
D72, H41, J13, J16.

Beyond the Tipping Point: Rent Feedback and Corruption Traps in Public-Sector Selection

Political Selection and Moral Frictions

Farina E., Rosso M., Dansero L., et al. (2023). Short-term effect of colorectal cancer on income: analysis of an Italian cohort. Journal of Epidemiology & Community Health, 77:196–201.

Introduction The ability to return to work after a cancer diagnosis is a key aspect of cancer survivorship and quality of life. Studies have reported a significant risk of income loss for cancer survivors; however, there is limited evidence of the Italian context.
Methods The Work Histories Italian Panel (WHIP)-Salute database was used to select a cohort of incident cases of colorectal cancer (CRC) among workers in the private sector, based on hospital discharges. A propensity score matching was used to find a balanced control group for several confounders. Ordinary least square and logistic regressions were used to estimate the effect of a CRC diagnosis on annual income and the probability of switching from a full-time contract to a part-time one considering 3 years after the diagnosis.
Results Overall, we identified 925 CRC incident cases from 2006 until 2012. Our results confirm a statistically significant reduction in survivors’ income compared with controls. This reduction was greater in the first year and then tend to decrease, with an average income loss over 3 years of about €12 000. Stratified analyses by sex and position confirmed the overall trend while indicating a strong effect modification. Regarding the switching from full-time to part-time employment, the results were never significant.
Conclusion Income loss does not seem to be related to an increase in part-time contracts, but rather to survivors’ reduced work capacity following the invasive treatments. Further research is needed to investigate the complex dynamics behind this association.
