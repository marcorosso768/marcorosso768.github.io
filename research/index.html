research

My research develops new theoretical models of incentives, information frictions, and selection, and brings their implications to the data. Empirically, I build reproducible pipelines that combine administrative records, web scraping and text analysis, and geocoding to study political participation, crime, and labor markets.

The Tipping Point of Temptation: Prevention, Sanctions, and Selection into Public Service

SSRN | CRENoS Working Paper 26/15 · ISBN 978-88-6851-718-2
Corruption opportunities attract people who would exploit them and repel people who anticipate the cost of resisting them. I study both margins in a model of occupational choice with self-control costs. Three results stand out. First, without sanctions, small corruption rents shrink the pool of entrants when less than half of the value of honest service is received at the moment of decision and the self-control conflict is strong enough relative to that share. Second, the public sector can lose average integrity while it shrinks, so a stable or falling headcount can conceal a deteriorating workforce. Third, at equal net corrupt surplus, removing corruption opportunities yields a weakly lower corrupt share than deferred sanctions, both at a given wage and with a fixed number of posts, although not necessarily higher average integrity. When rents rise with the share of corrupt incumbents, the ranking extends to steady states, and prevention can remove a high-corruption steady state that an equivalent sanction preserves.
occupational selection; self-control and temptation; corruption; deterrence; public-sector labor markets; multiple equilibria.

Who Misses the Election? Childbirth and Turnout within Couples

Request Draft
Childbirth is a temporary shock that hits two voters in one household. I ask how the resulting change in turnout is distributed within the couple. I use administrative turnout records for four elections in Bologna (2004–2013), where voting is in person on fixed days and the only alternative is a vote in hospital for the admitted patient, linked to registers that connect both parents to the same birth. I compare couples whose child is born just before the polling days with couples whose child is born just after. The loss of votes is concentrated in births in the last week before the vote. Relative to couples whose child is born in the four weeks after the vote, these couples cast 33.9 fewer votes per 100 couples (out of a possible 200), and the probability that both parents vote is 23.6 points lower; father-only voting and abstention by both parents are each about ten points more likely, while mother-only voting differs little. For births one to four weeks before the vote, the differences are small. A second, descriptive design follows couples around their first birth: in the first month after the birth their turnout is below their own pre-birth level, and from three months onward the gap between the mother’s and the father’s turnout is back near its pre-birth value. Accommodations for voters around childbirth should therefore be evaluated by their effect on the turnout of both parents.
voter turnout; voting costs; childbirth; couples; household; gender; political participation.
D72, J12, J13, J16.

Proximity to Public Childcare and Voter Turnout: Evidence from Bologna

joint with Giorgio Bellettini, Carlotta Berti Ceroni, Martín Gonzalez-Eiras, and Giovanni Prarolo
Request Draft
Does living near a locally provided public good raise the turnout of the households it serves? We study public childcare in Bologna, Italy, with administrative turnout records for four elections (2004–2013) linked to population registers and to a geocoded census of facilities. We follow parents with a single child from nursery to preschool to primary school, so that the facility that matters, and its distance from home, changes with the child’s age. If proximity leads parents to hold the municipality accountable, turnout should fall with distance to the municipal services (nursery and preschool) in local elections only, and not with distance to the state primary school. Comparing each parent with themselves, we detect no such gradient. Equivalence tests rule out effects larger than 0.56 percentage points per 100 meters (1.5 points over the interquartile range of distance), and hence effects as large as the previously documented 2.6-point turnout loss of mothers one to two years after a first birth, but not effects of one point. Within the precision of the data, proximity also leaves the turnout penalty of parenthood unchanged. When parents are also compared within polling precinct and election, turnout in local elections falls with distance to the state school and, among parents with an unchanged address, to the municipal services, significantly only in 2009, though not distinguishably from 2004. Because the state school shows the gradient too and the two tiers cannot be told apart, the design cannot attribute it to accountability. The estimates bound the effect of living near a facility, not of obtaining a place in it.
voter turnout; public goods; childcare; gender; local elections.
D72, H41, J13, J16.

Crime Perception and Voting Behavior: Evidence from Individual Data

joint with Giovanni Prarolo
This paper studies whether exposure to geolocated crime news shifts individual voting behavior in Italian elections. Using a panel of non-relocating Bologna voters observed across multiple election rounds, we exploit within-individual variation in exposure to nearby crime news during the pre-election month, holding constant individual and election fixed effects. Aggregate crime-news exposure yields weak and imprecise associations. Disaggregating by the offender’s perceived nationality reveals a sharp asymmetry. At national elections, exposure to immigrant-attributed crime news near a voter’s residence is associated with a within-individual decline of roughly 2 percentage points in support for the Five Star Movement—the largest party without a clear immigration stance during the sample period—and with a comparable rise in abstention; crime news attributed to Italian citizens carries no comparable association. The response is specific to crime content: comparable local non-crime news produces no analogous shift. These asymmetric responses, absent in aggregate measures, are more consistent with a nationality cue in crime reporting that activates an immigration-and-security frame than with a uniform reaction to crime salience. The evidence points to the attribution of crime in local news, rather than its mere occurrence, as the margin that matters for electoral behavior. A timing falsification supports the campaign-window reading but is sensitive to the offender-nationality classifier; we accordingly read the estimates as associations, with a cross-source replication under way to settle the causal interpretation.
crime; immigration; elections; news media; individual voting behavior.
D72, D83, K42, L82.

Generative AI and Posted Labor Demand: Volume, Seniority and Timing Submitted

joint with Fabio Cerina and Simone Nobili
CRENoS Working Paper 26/13 · ISBN 978-88-6851-699-4
Featured in: Lightcast (October 2026)
We study how the release of ChatGPT affected posted labor demand in the United States, separating the number of openings firms advertise from their seniority composition. Using 314 million job postings over 2016–2025 and a usage-anchored measure of LLM exposure, we compare occupations within the same sector, metropolitan market and month across four regimes: pre-pandemic, pandemic shock, pandemic recovery and post-release. From the recovery of March 2021–October 2022 to the post-release period, the gap in postings between top-quartile-exposed occupations and the rest widens by 6.8 log points. The contraction grows through 2025, survives remote-work controls, and is predicted by observed LLM usage over and above ex ante measures of what LLMs can do. The seniority composition had moved before the release: among postings our classifier assigns to a seniority tier, the junior share in exposed occupations fell by about 3.5 percentage points relative to the rest between early 2021 and the quarter before ChatGPT, with remote-work exposure accounting for about 1.5 of these points. After the release the share is 0.4 points below the last pre-release quarter, a difference that cannot be distinguished from zero. The junior share did decline in exposed occupations relative to the rest, but in the two years before the release; the measurable effect of generative AI to date is on the number of openings.
generative AI; ChatGPT; labor demand; seniority; job postings; difference-in-differences.

Labels Are Outcomes: Measuring Seniority in Job Postings around the Release of ChatGPT

joint with Fabio Cerina and Simone Nobili
Request Draft
Junior postings appear to decline in AI-exposed occupations, but seniority is read from text firms can reword without changing the job. A difference-in-differences estimate on a junior count mixes a change in jobs posted with a labeling component, from wording and classifier errors. The share of postings a classifier labels, its labeling rate, reveals only the component common to all tiers; the tier-specific part needs human labels on a sample stratified by exposure group, period and assigned tier, unlabeled postings included. We give an estimator and label allocation. On 314 million U.S. postings, with tier mix unchanged, the largest yearly fall in a rule-based classifier’s labeling rate after ChatGPT puts this component at about −0.7 log points on the application panel (−1.5 on a coarser panel, where stated years give +5 from the pandemic on). With 252 human labels the corrected junior-share change has no stable sign across LLM readings; simulations call for thousands of labels.
job postings; text-derived measurement; seniority; entry-level work; generative AI; large language models; partial identification.
C81, J23, J24, O33.

Farina E., Rosso M., Dansero L., et al. (2023). Short-term effect of colorectal cancer on income: analysis of an Italian cohort. Journal of Epidemiology & Community Health, 77:196–201.

Introduction The ability to return to work after a cancer diagnosis is a key aspect of cancer survivorship and quality of life. Studies have reported a significant risk of income loss for cancer survivors; however, there is limited evidence of the Italian context.
Methods The Work Histories Italian Panel (WHIP)-Salute database was used to select a cohort of incident cases of colorectal cancer (CRC) among workers in the private sector, based on hospital discharges. A propensity score matching was used to find a balanced control group for several confounders. Ordinary least square and logistic regressions were used to estimate the effect of a CRC diagnosis on annual income and the probability of switching from a full-time contract to a part-time one considering 3 years after the diagnosis.
Results Overall, we identified 925 CRC incident cases from 2006 until 2012. Our results confirm a statistically significant reduction in survivors’ income compared with controls. This reduction was greater in the first year and then tend to decrease, with an average income loss over 3 years of about €12 000. Stratified analyses by sex and position confirmed the overall trend while indicating a strong effect modification. Regarding the switching from full-time to part-time employment, the results were never significant.
Conclusion Income loss does not seem to be related to an increase in part-time contracts, but rather to survivors’ reduced work capacity following the invasive treatments. Further research is needed to investigate the complex dynamics behind this association.
