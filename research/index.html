The Tipping Point of Temptation: Prevention, Sanctions, and Selection into Public Service

SSRN | CRENoS Working Paper 26/15 · ISBN 978-88-6851-718-2
A chance to take a bribe attracts the people who would take it and repels the people who would have to keep refusing it. I study both effects in a model of occupational choice in which the cost of resisting responds to the bribe, received now, and not to a sanction that comes later. First, consider a bribe without sanctions, and suppose some people with almost no integrity are close to indifferent about entering. A small bribe then reduces the number of people who want a public job when the share of the value of honesty felt at the moment of choice is low enough, and the threshold depends, to leading order, on the two parameters of temptation, not on the distribution of types. Second, size does not reveal composition: where a marginal increase in the bribe leaves the headcount unchanged, it replaces honest entrants with corrupt ones, and close to that point the sector can shrink and worsen together. Third, for reforms that lower the net gain from corruption by the same amount, removing the opportunity leaves a corrupt share no higher than a deferred sanction does, at a given wage and with a fixed number of posts; with fixed posts, a cost of resisting that is linear or convex, and small reforms, letting people choose whether to enter can only shrink the relative advantage of prevention. With bribes that rise with the share of corrupt incumbents, the ranking extends to steady states, and prevention can remove a high-corruption steady state that an equal sanction preserves.
occupational selection; self-control and temptation; corruption; deterrence; public-sector labor markets; multiple equilibria.
D73, J45, D91, H83.

Who Misses the Election? Childbirth and Turnout within Couples

Request Draft
Childbirth is a temporary shock that hits two voters in one household. I ask how the resulting change in turnout is distributed within the couple. I use administrative turnout records for four elections in Bologna (2004–2013), linked to population registers that connect both parents to the same birth. Voting is in person on fixed days, and the only accommodation around childbirth is a vote in hospital for the admitted patient. I compare couples whose child is born just before the polling days with couples whose child is born just after. The loss of votes comes mostly from births in the last week before the vote. Relative to couples whose child is born in the four weeks after the vote, these couples cast 33.9 fewer votes per 100 couples, and the probability that both parents vote is 23.6 points lower; father-only voting and abstention by both parents are each about ten points more likely, while mother-only voting differs little; the rise in couples with no voter is in line with what the two parents’ losses imply if the link between their decisions is unchanged. For births eight to 28 days before the vote, the differences are much smaller. A second, descriptive design follows couples around their first birth: in the first month after the birth their turnout is below their own pre-birth level, and from three months onward the gap between the mother’s and the father’s turnout is back near its pre-birth value, while joint voting stays slightly below it. Accommodations for voters around childbirth should therefore be evaluated by their effect on the turnout of both parents.
voter turnout; voting costs; childbirth; couples; household; gender; political participation.
D72, J12, J13, J16.

Proximity to Public Childcare and Voter Turnout: Evidence from Bologna

joint with Giorgio Bellettini, Carlotta Berti Ceroni, Martín Gonzalez-Eiras, and Giovanni Prarolo
Request Draft
Does living near a locally provided public good raise the turnout of the households it serves? We study public childcare in Bologna, Italy, with administrative turnout records for four elections (2004–2013) linked to population registers and to a geocoded census of facilities. We follow parents with a single child from nursery to preschool to primary school, so that the facility that matters, and its distance from home, changes with the child’s age. If proximity leads parents to hold the municipality accountable, turnout should fall with distance to the municipal services (nursery and preschool) in local elections only, and not with distance to the state primary school. In the baseline, which follows each parent across elections, turnout does not fall with distance. Equivalence tests rule out effects larger than 0.56 percentage points per 100 meters, or 1.5 points over the interquartile range of distance: over that range, effects as large as the previously documented 2.6-point turnout loss of mothers one to two years after a first birth are ruled out. Effects of one point are not. Within the precision of the data, proximity also leaves the turnout penalty of parenthood unchanged. When parents are also compared within polling precinct and election, turnout in local elections falls with distance to the state school and, among parents with an unchanged address, to the municipal services. This fall is significant only in 2009, though not distinguishably from 2004. Because the state school shows it too and the municipal and state tiers cannot be told apart, the design can neither attribute it to accountability nor rule that reading out. The estimates bound the effect of living near a facility, not of obtaining a place in it.
voter turnout; public goods; childcare; gender; local elections.
D72, H41, J13, J16.

Crime Perception and Voting Behavior: Evidence from Individual Data

joint with Giovanni Prarolo
This paper studies how exposure to geolocated crime-related news shapes individual voting behavior in Italian elections. Using a panel of non-relocating voters observed across multiple election rounds, we exploit within-individual variation in exposure to nearby crime news during the pre-election month, controlling for individual and district-by-election fixed effects. Aggregate exposure yields weak, unstable effects. Disaggregating by offender nationality reveals systematic patterns: immigrant-attributed crime news reduces support for parties with ambiguous immigration stances (e.g., Five Star Movement) and increases support for clear “law-and-order” parties, while Italian-attributed crime has negligible effects. Effects are stronger among high-skilled voters shifting from M5S and low-skilled voters abandoning Lega. In local elections, Italian crime punishes incumbents, while immigrant crime increases abstention. These asymmetric responses—absent in aggregate measures—indicate that crime salience operates primarily through identity-based framing rather than through generalized concerns about crime or security. The findings highlight how media attribution shapes electoral accountability.
crime; immigration; elections; news media; individual voting behavior.
D72, D83, K42, L82.

Generative AI and Posted Labor Demand: Volume, Seniority and Timing

joint with Fabio Cerina and Simone Nobili
CRENoS Working Paper 26/13 · ISBN 978-88-6851-699-4
Featured in: Lightcast (October 2026)
Submitted
We study how the release of ChatGPT affected posted labor demand in the United States, separating the number of openings firms advertise from their seniority composition. Using 314 million job postings over 2016–2025 and a usage-anchored measure of LLM exposure, we compare occupations within the same sector, metropolitan market and month across four regimes: pre-pandemic, pandemic shock, pandemic recovery and post-release. From the recovery of March 2021–October 2022 to the post-release period, the gap in postings between top-quartile-exposed occupations and the rest widens by 6.8 log points. The contraction grows through 2025, survives remote-work controls, and is predicted by observed LLM usage over and above ex ante measures of what LLMs can do. The seniority composition had moved before the release: among postings our classifier assigns to a seniority tier, the junior share in exposed occupations fell by about 3.5 percentage points relative to the rest between early 2021 and the quarter before ChatGPT, with remote-work exposure accounting for about 1.5 of these points. After the release the share is 0.4 points below the last pre-release quarter, a difference that cannot be distinguished from zero. The junior share did decline in exposed occupations relative to the rest, but in the two years before the release; the measurable effect of generative AI to date is on the number of openings.
generative AI; ChatGPT; labor demand; seniority; job postings; difference-in-differences.
J23, J24, O33, M51.

Labels Are Outcomes: Measuring Seniority in Job Postings around the Release of ChatGPT

joint with Fabio Cerina and Simone Nobili
Request Draft
A label read from the text of a job posting is an outcome: firms can change the wording without changing the position. Seniority is such a label, so a difference-in-differences estimate on a labeled junior count mixes a posted-demand component with a labeling component, from wording and classifier errors. The share of postings a classifier labels identifies at most the common labeling component; the rest requires reference labels in every cell of treatment group, period and assigned tier, unlabeled postings included. We give an estimator and an allocation rule favoring large, noisy strata. On 314 million U.S. postings, a rule-based classifier’s labeling rate moves by under one log point on the application panel after ChatGPT’s release, not distinguishably from zero; on an occupation-by-industry panel, a stated-years rate reproduced from the text rises by about 5 log points from the pandemic on. With 252 human labels, the corrected junior-share change is undetermined: its sign depends on the model reading used as input. In simulations, fixed classifier errors produce a tier-specific component of about −15 log points at assumed error rates, and usual-size validation samples rarely tell a relabeling from none.
job postings; text-derived measurement; seniority; entry-level work; generative AI; large language models; partial identification.
C81, J23, J24, O33.
