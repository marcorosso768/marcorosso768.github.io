She was about four, and I had something hard to explain: that even when I have to leave, my heart stays there, with her and her mom. And lately, with the little sister on the way, too.

The trouble is that, for a child, time doesn’t work the way it does for us. “Tomorrow” and “in five days” are the same vague amount of time. And so is “yesterday.”

> Can we play like yesterday, when you pretended to fall?

That “yesterday,” almost innocent, meant three months earlier.

The drawing, though, worked. She usually takes drawings down after a few days. This one has been on the wall, in the same spot, for over a year, and she has never touched it.

So I thought: if time can’t be explained, maybe it can be shown. And instead of buying a paper calendar, I coded one.

It’s called **Magic Calendar**, it’s a desktop app for Mac and Windows, and it’s free.

## We’re here, my heart is here

The heart of the app, literally, is two kinds of day:

- **“We’re here!”**: the days we’re together;
- **“My heart is here”**: the days I’m away, but my heart stays with them.

Two colors, two emoji, and a kind of time that can finally be counted: the little squares left until the next “We’re here!”

Then the thing happened that always happens to projects started for fun: someone began using it for real. A friend with two five-year-olds uses it to keep track of their weekly activities. And a calendar made for one little girl became a calendar **for families**.

## What it does (in short)

_For those who just want to know if they need it_

- Monthly and weekly views, with themes, emoji, and your own images.
- Day types you invent: together, away, school, grandparents, whatever you need.
- School days and holidays in different colors, long weekends, and your town’s patron saint’s day. And now you can import the school calendar straight from an `.ics` file, if your school publishes one.
- Select several days at once and set repeating patterns (every other week, too), with Undo for when you get carried away.
- A countdown to the next day of each type. _(Yes, it’s the most important feature of all.)_
- PDF export, to print and stick on the fridge, and export to Apple Calendar or Google Calendar.
- Everything stays on your computer: no accounts, no servers, no data collected.

## A spaceship in outer space

This isn’t the first time my work has crossed paths with children.

With **<a href="https://site.unibo.it/minuto/en">MinUTo</a>**, a University of Bologna project on mindful parenting, I ran online experiments with children. We pretended to be on a spaceship in outer space. I watched them fly off on their imagination, be patient (or not), push their luck, and be disappointed.

I watched painfully shy kids change in less than forty minutes: trust, open up. And I met kids so excited that they gave me little gifts, or a drawing.

Later, I also worked on the rollout of the [MinUTo web app](https://www.appminuto.it/)[^languages], bringing it to schools and families.

What stayed with me is this: with children, what works is what they can see, touch, and count. Magic Calendar, in its small way, comes from the same idea.

## What I learned

- **“Just for fun” is the most dangerous phrase in software.** From 1.0 to 1.7 is a short trip, and every version came with its own request: “what if I could mark school days too?”, “what if I could print it?”
- **Automatic updates beat any feature.** Nobody goes to GitHub to download a new version on their own. Now the app updates itself: you publish, it replaces itself.

## Where to get it

The app is on [GitHub](https://github.com/marcorosso768/magic-calendar): just download the latest version for your computer. It’s free for personal and educational use.

If you use it and find a bug, open an issue on GitHub or leave a comment below. And if you use it and don’t find any bugs, write anyway: that doesn’t happen often.

## TL;DR?

For a child, “tomorrow” and “three months from now” are the same thing.<br>
**A calendar doesn’t make the wait shorter, but it makes it visible. And once you can see it, it’s less scary.**
