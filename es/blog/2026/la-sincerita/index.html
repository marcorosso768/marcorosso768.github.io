La sincerità? Santa Maradona, still a generation’s portrait twenty-five years on

Publicado el 14 de noviembre de 2026

2026   ·   film   personal   familia

I had just finished my bachelor’s in Mathematics and was about to start a master’s in Economics. In between, I had an interview with a consulting firm, for a position in the software development unit.

During the interview came the inevitable question: your greatest strength, your worst weakness. I answered la sincerità, sincerity, both times.

I had always wanted to quote Santa Maradona in an interview, and that day, strangely enough, I did. But first, the film.

The film

Santa Maradona is a film by Marco Ponti, released in 2001. It is set among disillusioned young graduates in Turin, a generation that keeps missing the target, then as now. It follows Andrea (Stefano Accorsi), who drifts from one job interview to the next. Bart (Libero De Rienzo), his flatmate, calls himself a literary critic: a cousin in Sicily emails him reviews from a local paper, and Bart signs and sends them on without reading either the book or the review. They share a flat, and before long being precarious becomes almost something to boast about:

Andrea: The rent is off the books. We don’t work, we’re not on any unemployment register… technically, we don’t exist!
Bart: I’d say it with a bit less pride, though.

The film opens with one of Andrea’s interviews:

Employer: What would you say is your greatest strength?
Andrea: Sincerity.
Employer: And your main weakness?
Andrea: Sincerity.
Employer: What is your highest aspiration?
Andrea: Sincerity.
Employer: You’re a sharp young man, I see. So tell me: what is your family’s annual income?
Andrea: Sincerity?

A hundred times

I first saw it in high school, in bits and pieces, on the tiny screen of an iPod video. I must have watched it a hundred times since, and I know the lines by heart.

One of my favourite scenes is in a bookshop. Bart is explaining that he can’t stand books because they’re too expensive, when a customer, who has mistaken him for a shop assistant, asks him for help. She has mixed up two titles that, in Italian, sound almost the same: Stefano Benni’s La compagnia dei celestini and James Redfield’s The Celestine Prophecy (La profezia di Celestino):

Well, madam, I’m afraid there’s a slight confusion about the title. So, you’re confusing La compagnia dei celestini, Stefano Benni, Feltrinelli, with La profezia di Celestino, James Redfield, Corbaccio? Corbaccio. Calm down, excuse me a moment, please. You should know that James Redfield’s book, La profezia di Celestino, is a crappy little new age book, and we don’t stock crappy little new age books here. Did you hear my colleague? Whereas La compagnia dei celestini is a bestseller, and we don’t stock bestsellers here, we only have quality books. Is that clear? There you go, thank you, madam, make yourself comfortable, that’s the door.

But the scene almost everyone remembers is the run. It’s a game among friends. Dolores, one of the group, is wearing a wedding dress; she pretends that Andrea only wants to marry her because she’s pregnant, and runs off in tears. The others chase after her to bring her back, and instead they just run, laughing, through the ground-level fountains of Piazza Castello.

For anyone who grew up in Turin, that square means something specific: on the last day of high school, we all met there, whatever school we came from, for water fights. Watching that run takes me back there.

Australia

Throughout the film, Andrea’s safety net is a job in his uncle’s family business, which sells shower cubicles: the emblem of settling for less. In the final scene, Andrea and Bart are watching Butch Cassidy and the Sundance Kid on TV:

Andrea: What if it really is too late?
Bart: What do you think?
Andrea: I don’t know. Let’s turn everything off and get far away from here.
Bart: And go where?
Butch Cassidy (on TV): Australia.
Andrea: Australia.
Bart: Australia?
Andrea: Yes, Australia.
Bart: Australia’s no better than here, Butch.
Andrea: Australia, Sundance. Endless places to hide. Good weather, good sea… think about it.
Bart: All right, I’ll think about it.
[…]
Bart: And now? Shower cubicle?
Andrea: No. Now let’s try to fix things.

My partner and I always say “Australia” whenever we plan our future, elsewhere, anywhere, together. The film means a lot to us. Our first purchase as a couple was a reproduction of the original script, with the shooting notes and stills. It was a limited edition, and the proceeds went to the Candiolo cancer research centre.1

The target

In the end, they offered me a position, in Milan or in Turin. I turned it down. Not because I didn’t like programming: I liked it then and I still do, and programming languages have always fascinated me. But after years of mathematics I wasn’t ready to stop studying. The master’s took me down another road: to the PhD, to research, to my partner, and to where I am now and the people I share it with.

Today, a quarter of a century after the film came out, I’m on the academic job market. This time, the job I’m looking for is the one I chose back then, by turning down the other.

Santa Maradona is the portrait of a generation that kept missing the target: interviews that went nowhere, CVs nobody answered, irony as a shield. Some things haven’t changed: most contracts have an end date, mine included. But I have a partner I love and who loves me, a daughter who isn’t mine by blood but is in every other way, and another one due in January. And I’m enjoying every bit of it. When I think of my family, I believe I hit the target.

And if someone asked me today about my strengths and weaknesses, I would still answer la sincerità.

TL;DR?

I quoted Santa Maradona in a job interview, and then turned that job down.
I hit the target once. Whatever comes next, I’m still aiming.
