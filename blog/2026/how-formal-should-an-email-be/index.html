How formal should an email be?

Created in September 30, 2026

2026   ·   academia   email

Every professor has received this email:

Can you tell me where the exam is held?

(Spoiler: most universities have a dedicated page with this information, updated with near-religious devotion. And if that’s not enough, there’s the student office. Worth a look before writing.)

Every year, thousands of students email their professors. And very often—not out of malice, but out of inexperience—the result is a small communicative disaster: awkward greetings, insurance-contract phrasing, missing punctuation.

So, taking inspiration from “Poche semplici regole per scrivere una e-mail ad un professore” (A few simple rules for writing an email to a professor) by Professor Davide Dragone, here’s my personal take on how to write good emails to faculty. Because between overly formal wording, impersonal verbs, and the panic of not remembering whether the professor told me to use their first name… I’ve struggled too.

Let’s start with some real examples—from ultra-formal to… yikes.

Sample emails (from most formal to… let’s say “less ideal”)

1. The perfectly formal one

For those aiming to impress either the Academy or the Royal Family

Subject: Econometrics: slides for lecture 10
Dear Professor Lecter,
My name is C-3PO, and I am enrolled in your Econometrics course.
I am writing to ask whether the slides for lecture 10 will be made available online.
Thank you in advance for your time.
Best regards,
C-3PO

  • Pros: flawless, clear, professional.
  • Cons: could’ve been written by a highly educated chatbot.
  • Reader’s reaction: approval… mixed with slight existential boredom.

2. The balanced approach

Human, polite, effective—my favorite

Subject: Econometrics: slides for lecture 10
Dear Professor Lecter,
I’m C-3PO, a student in your Econometrics course. I was wondering if the slides for lecture 10 will be uploaded to the course page soon.
Thanks in advance, and have a great day!
C-3PO

  • Pros: direct, respectful, perfect in most cases.
  • Cons: none. Save it as a draft and reuse forever.
  • Reader’s reaction: smile, fast reply, instant respect.

3. The “yeah, fine” email

Casual but respectful. Aka: normal.

Hi Professor,
I couldn’t find the slides for lecture 10 on the course page. Do you know if they’ll be uploaded soon?
Thanks a lot!
C-3PO

  • Pros: simple, practical, human.
  • Cons: better avoided on first contact.
  • Reader’s reaction: fine, but maybe not the best first impression.

4. The “text message from the void”

A kind of teenage S.O.S.

Hi prof u got the slides 4 lecture 10? need asap

  • Pros: none.
  • Cons: all of them.
  • Reader’s reaction: confusion, then a long pause before replying.

5. The “yo prof” vibe

Yes, it exists. No, it shouldn’t.

Yo prof all good? Quick thing: when r u posting the slides for lecture 10? Btw the course is 🔥 see ya in class ✌️

  • Pros: potential TikTok virality.
  • Cons: not the tone for an email to someone you’ll ask for a recommendation letter one day.
  • Reader’s reaction: shock, resignation, existential crisis.

If you want to be formal…

  • Subject: Make it say what the email is about, course included: “Econometrics: slides for lecture 10”, not “Question” (and never empty).
  • Greeting: “Hi Prof” isn’t formal. It’s the email version of a shrug. Go with “Dear Professor Lecter”: title and surname.
  • Content: Introduce yourself (name, course) and get to the point. Avoid stuff like “I hereby write to inquire regarding…”—this isn’t a Victorian novel.
  • Before writing: Check the syllabus and the course page. Half of the answers are already there.
  • Address: Write from your university account. Emails from “xXdarklordXx@…” tend to end up in spam, or worse, get read.
  • Style: Polite, concise, and with real punctuation. And please, skip the passive-aggressive “Awaiting your reply.”
  • Timing: Write whenever you like, but send during working hours. “Schedule send” is your friend. Use it. You’ll thank yourself.

…but is all this formality always necessary?

Let’s be honest: these rules are useful, but they’re not the only way to write a good email.

You can be clear, respectful, and kind without sounding like a contract. The right tone depends on the context, your relationship with the professor, the type of university—and, let’s be honest, the country.

That’s why an email like this one:

Hi Marco,
I’m having trouble finding the slides for lecture 10. Do you know if they’ll be posted soon?
Thanks a lot, talk soon!
C-3PO

is, for me, perfectly fine. Not formal, but respectful. And at the end of the day, isn’t that what really matters?

TL;DR?

If you want to write a formal email, do it properly.
But if formality isn’t expected, there are simpler, friendlier, and still respectful alternatives.

Because, in the end, politeness beats formalism. Always.
